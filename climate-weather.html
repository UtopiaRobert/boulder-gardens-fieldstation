Land & Ecology · Climate record

Climate &
Weather

Weather writes directly on the desert.

Boulder Gardens sits in the high desert of the Mojave, where long dry periods are interrupted by winter Pacific storms, summer monsoon moisture, strong winds, heat, cold, and occasional bursts of rain. Keeping a climate record helps connect those events to runoff, plants, wildlife, soil, and the sanctuary’s water systems.

Regional reference

A dry place, measured over time

The closest long historical reference used here is the Joshua Tree climate station. Boulder Gardens has its own elevation and microclimate, so these figures are context—not a claim that conditions are identical at the sanctuary.

Notable weather event · September 6, 2026

A September rain larger than the monthly average

A nearby Pioneertown station recorded 0.44 inches of precipitation on September 6. That is more than the Joshua Tree station’s historical 0.35-inch average for the entire month of September.

The same Pioneertown station recorded a high of 85°F, a low of 70°F, and a maximum wind speed of 13 mph. Desert rainfall can vary dramatically over short distances, so the 0.44-inch figure should be read as a nearby gauge observation rather than an exact Boulder Gardens total.

Seasonal patterns

Weather that shapes the sanctuary

Summer

Monsoon & tropical moisture

Thunderstorms can be brief, intense, and extraordinarily local. A storm may soak one ridge or wash while leaving another nearly dry.

Cool season

Pacific storms

Winter systems provide an important share of the region’s rainfall and can bring prolonged cloud cover, colder temperatures, and occasional dramatic runoff.

Year-round

Wind, heat & cold

Rain is only part of the climate story. Wind, temperature swings, evaporation, frost, and extreme heat all shape how water and life persist here.

Ecology in motion

What happens after rain

A desert storm is not just weather. It becomes hydrology, plant response, animal activity, erosion, storage, and eventually another layer in the field record.

01

Ephemeral water

Runoff moves quickly through rock basins, washes, paths, and low places. Temporary pools make the shape of the watershed visible.

02

Plants respond

Even modest rainfall can change soil moisture and trigger new growth, flowering, germination, or recovery after heat stress.

03

Wildlife follows

Water and plant growth create short-lived opportunities for insects, birds, reptiles, mammals, and other desert life.

Fieldstation notes

Building a local climate record

The regional station record provides historical context. The Fieldstation’s longer-term goal is more local: pair weather events with photographs, sensor measurements, water-system observations, and ecological notes from Boulder Gardens itself.

  • Record notable rain, wind, heat, cold, and storm events.
  • Document runoff and water-harvesting behavior across the property.
  • Pair repeat photography with seasonal observations.
  • Preserve local measurements as a durable long-term archive.
